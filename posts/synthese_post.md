title: "Incidence as Foundation: A Relational Ontology for Mathematics and Science"
journal: "Synthese"

### Abstract

The foundations of mathematics have historically relied on an ontology that sharply distinguishes objects (elements, points, things) from the relations between them. This paper argues that this object-relation dualism, inherited from classical metaphysics and formalized in set theory, is a contingent choice rather than a necessary one. We propose a formal alternative, the Theory of Incidence (RIF), which serves as a proof-of-concept for a purely relational ontology.

In RIF, `incidences`—primitive, self-referential relations—are the sole ontological entity. What we perceive as "objects" are shown to be emergent, stable patterns within the relational fabric: either zero-ary incidences (atomic individuals) or fixed-points of relational configurations (self-maintaining processes). This framework provides a direct formalization of concepts from process philosophy (A.N. Whitehead), where reality is constituted by events and their interconnections, not by static substances.

Furthermore, we connect RIF to the Buddhist philosophical concept of *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent origination), arguing that the theory's recursive definition of incidences provides a mathematical model for a universe without a ground, where every entity exists only in dependence on others. By challenging the primacy of objects, RIF offers a new formal language for philosophy of science, particularly for theories in physics, biology, and cognitive science where dynamic processes and network structures are more fundamental than their constituent "parts." It suggests that a relational turn in our mathematical foundations can yield a more unified and dynamic picture of reality.
